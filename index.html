Annotated Bibliography For Using GIS to Assess Habitat Suitability for Raptor Species in Los Altos, California

Written and Organized by Carl van Reis for the Winter Term 2026 term of GEOG 560 at Oregon State University

This collection of scientific literature and web sources are intended to inform how one would approach using Geographic Information Systems (GIS) to assess how suitable the semi-urban landscape of Los Altos, CA is to the continued presence of raptor species observed in its boundaries and in surrounding areas as one means of judging the area's ability to support an intricate food web.

Annotated Bibligraophy

Armstrong, T.V. (2023). “Hawk Poop: Everything You’ve Ever Wanted To Know.” A-Z Animals. https://a-z-animals.com/blog/hawk-poop-everything-youve-ever-wanted-to-know/

Summary In this blog post for A-Z animals, Armstrong takes a comprehensive deep-dive into hawk poop and what we can learn from it. She first covers what makes the hawk genera different in their hunting and prey choices to inform some of the contributing differences between hawks. Afterwards, she answers a wide array of questions that could come up regarding hawk feces, including color, location, smell, and danger level.

Relevance to Capstone From the list of prey the author provides on the buteos genus—which red-shouldered hawks belong to—rabbits, rats, squirrels, insects, and small birds emerge as the potential prey for red-shouldered hawks in Los Altos, California. The author notes that red-shouldered hawks prefer to hunt in open areas, and roadways are included in that category, making an urban environment potentially useful for these hawks albeit dangerous. The piece also makes a distinction between the red-shouldered hawk and Cooper’s hawks which also inhabit the area, with Cooper’s Hawk belonging to the Accipiters genus, which prefers to hunt lower to the ground than buteos. Accipiters she says, prefer to prey on songbirds, which would suggest these two hawks are filling separate niches in the habitat. The blog post also highlights pellets from hawks having less bones than from owls, marking an indicator that can be use for estimating hawk and owl range and nest location in Los Altos. Back to the topic of the feces itself, hawk feces can superficially look similar to that of many other birds that coat their feces in a white substance. However, different colors can provide clues about hunting success; green feces could indicate a struggle to eat while yellow would mean they have eaten fat-rich prey recently. However, these colors could also be a sign of an infection too, confounding this identification method for untrained parties. The hawk penchant for defecating upon takeoff versus in-flight adds to the strength of feces as an indicator of nesting, hunting, or resting spots within a range, with babies sometimes only defecting as far down as a section of their inhabited tree.
“Red-shouldered Hawk.” (n.d.). Hawk Mountain Sanctuary. https://www.hawkmountain.org/raptors/red-shouldered-hawk Summary This profile of red-shouldered hawks covers fun facts, how to identify them, mating rituals, their diet, migration habits, and conservation status. Relevance to Capstone This source was sought out to answer the question of nest size for these hawks as one potential rule for determining suitable nesting sites, with the high-end size being 2 feet across and 1 foot deep. The same section mentions that they build their nests in the upper half of trees and often near the tree’s center. This detail may be difficult to use in practice with GIS but could be a building block for favoring trees with wider canopies. The webpage mentions that the Great-Horned Owl is a threat to this species at every life stage, particularly going after the hawk’s eggs. Raccoons are mentioned as another egg snatcher. These species could be seen as important natural means of long term checks to potential hawk overabundance in urban settings to factor in when thinking through the larger ecosystem.
